Inheritance Advance Companies: How to Compare Offers

What to ask, what to read in the contract, and which California rules protect you before you sign with any inheritance funding company.

Published October 20, 2026

If you are waiting on a California probate case, you have probably seen ads from several inheritance advance companies. Their offers can look alike, but two offers with the same check amount can take very different amounts out of your inheritance, and some contracts include terms California law does not allow. Here is how to compare offers side by side.

What an inheritance funding company actually does

An inheritance funding company pays you cash now in exchange for an assignment of part of your share of the estate. When the estate distributes, the agreed amount goes to the company directly from your share, and you keep the rest. It is not a loan. If you are new to the idea, start with what an inheritance advance is and how it differs from a probate loan.

Compare the total cost, not the check you receive

The single most useful number in any offer is the gap between two figures:

  • The cash you receive (the advance amount), and
  • The amount assigned to the company from your inheritance at distribution.

That gap is the true cost. A bigger check paired with a much bigger slice of your inheritance can cost you more than a smaller, cleaner offer. Put every offer in one table: cash today, amount assigned, and the difference.

Fixed fee or a fee that grows over time?

Probate in California often takes 9 to 18 months, and delays are common. Some funders use a fixed fee that never changes. Others use a schedule where the amount they take steps up the longer the estate stays open.

Ask each company directly: If this estate takes a year longer than expected, will I owe you more? At Cash4ProbateHomes, our fee is fixed up front, so a slow court does not cost you more, and we charge no application fee and no upfront fees.

Check for hidden and upfront fees

California requires an inheritance purchase agreement to state the total of all costs or fees charged to you because of the transfer. That includes processing, credit report, title search, due diligence, filing, and transfer costs. When you compare offers, ask for that total in writing and add it to the true cost. Be wary of any company that wants money from you before you are funded.

Make sure the advance is truly non-recourse

Creditor claims, a drop in property value, unpaid taxes, or a will contest can all shrink what you inherit. With a non-recourse advance, that risk belongs to the funding company. If the estate comes up short, you do not owe the difference out of your own pocket.

California Probate Code section 11604.5 makes any provision in these agreements that gives the company recourse against you for a shortfall void, except for losses caused by your own fraud or material breach of the agreement. Even so, read the contract. If it says you must repay, guarantee, or make up any shortfall, treat that as a red flag. California rules that protect heirs: Under Probate Code section 11604.5, a company that regularly buys inheritance interests must file the signed agreement with the probate court, serve notice on the personal representative, and print the documents in at least 10-point type. The agreement must state the cash paid, the interest transferred, and the total fees. The court can refuse to enforce an agreement whose fees are grossly unreasonable or that was obtained by duress, fraud, or undue influence.

Read the contract for terms California does not allow

Section 11604.5 also voids certain provisions outright. If an offer includes any of these, it is a sign the company is not following California rules:

  • A clause holding the company harmless for anything other than your own fraud.
  • A clause giving the company power to represent your interest in the estate beyond the share you assigned.
  • A requirement to pay the company for services unrelated to the advance.
  • Recourse against you if the estate pays less than the amount assigned.

Questions to ask every inheritance advance company

  1. How much cash will I receive, and exactly how much of my inheritance will you take at distribution?
  2. Is the fee fixed, or does it increase if probate takes longer?
  3. What is the total of all fees and costs, and do I pay anything before I am funded?
  4. Is the advance non-recourse if the estate is worth less than expected?
  5. Will you file the agreement with the probate court and notify the executor as California requires?
  6. How long does review take, and when will funds arrive after I accept?

A good company answers all of these in writing without pressure. Vague answers or a push to sign today are reasons to keep shopping.

How to read probate advance reviews

Look for a pattern in reviews, not a single glowing or angry one: did the amount taken at distribution match what the heir was promised, were there surprise fees, and how were delays handled? Reviews never replace reading the agreement, since its written terms are what the court and the estate follow.

Do you need the best offer or just a good fit?

The best inheritance advance company for you is the one with the lowest true cost, a fixed fee, no upfront charges, real non-recourse terms, and clear compliance with California law. Inheritance advance company FAQ

How do I compare inheritance advance offers fairly?

Compare the amount each company takes from your inheritance at distribution against the cash you receive today. Then check whether the fee is fixed or grows over time, add any other fees, and confirm the advance is non-recourse.

Is an inheritance advance company the same as a lender?

No. An inheritance advance is an assignment of part of your inheritance, not a loan. There is no interest, no monthly payment, and no credit check.

Does California regulate inheritance advance companies?

Yes. Probate Code section 11604.5 sets rules for companies that regularly buy inheritance interests, including filing the agreement with the court, disclosing all fees, and banning recourse against heirs for a shortfall. The court can also refuse to enforce grossly unreasonable terms.

Do I need the executor's approval to get an advance?

No. You are assigning part of your own share, so you do not need the executor's or the other heirs' approval. The executor is notified of the assignment so the estate can pay it at distribution.
