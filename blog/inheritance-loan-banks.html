Inheritance Loan vs. Advance: Which Banks Actually Lend?

Why a regular bank usually will not lend against an inheritance you have not received yet, what bank options heirs really have, and how an inheritance advance is different.

Published November 17, 2026

If you are waiting on an estate in California, one of the first ideas is to call your bank and ask for an inheritance loan. It seems reasonable: you know money is coming, so why not borrow against it? This guide explains how traditional banks usually look at an expected inheritance, which bank products heirs may still be able to use, and how an inheritance advance works differently.

What banks do inheritance loans?

Generally speaking, traditional banks and credit unions do not offer a loan secured by an inheritance that is still going through probate. You may see the phrase "inheritance loan" in ads, but those offers usually come from specialty probate funding companies, not from the bank where you keep your checking account.

Policies vary by lender and can change, so it never hurts to ask your own bank. Just expect that a standard loan officer will look at your income, credit, and assets you already own, not at a share of an estate you have not received.

Why banks usually will not lend against an expected inheritance

A bank loan works because the lender can measure the risk and, for a secured loan, take a lien on something you own. An inheritance in probate does not fit that model well:

  • You do not own the property yet. While probate is open, estate assets are controlled by the court-appointed personal representative (the executor or administrator). You cannot give a bank a lien on a house that is still titled to the estate.
  • The amount is uncertain. Your final share depends on what is left after debts, taxes, probate costs, and any disputes. A bank cannot easily underwrite a number that may change.
  • The timing is uncertain. California probate often takes 9 to 18 months or longer. Banks prefer a clear repayment date.
  • It is outside normal products. Reviewing probate files and court filings is specialized work that most banks do not do.

The executor can sometimes borrow for the estate with court approval, but that money serves the estate, not one heir. See probate loans in California for how that works.

Bank options heirs may actually have

Even if your bank will not lend against the inheritance itself, you may still qualify for regular credit based on your own finances. These are ordinary loans, so they come with the usual credit review and repayment duties.

  • An unsecured personal loan. Approval and terms depend on your credit score, income, and existing debt. The expected inheritance is not collateral, and you owe monthly payments no matter how the estate turns out.
  • A HELOC or cash-out refinance on a home you already own. If you own your own house with equity, a bank may lend against it. This puts your own home on the line, and you repay on the lender's schedule.
  • A loan after distribution. Once probate closes and the court orders distribution, inherited real estate can be deeded into your name. After title is in your name, you own the property and can apply for a mortgage, refinance, or HELOC on it like any other owner, subject to normal approval.

The common thread: banks lend based on what you own and what you earn today. If you have strong credit and equity, a bank product may cost less. If you do not, or you need money before distribution, a bank is often not the answer.

Inheritance advance loans: how an advance is different

Many products marketed as "inheritance advance loans" are not loans at all. With an inheritance advance, you assign part of your expected share to a funding company in exchange for cash now. The company is paid from your share when the estate distributes.

  • No credit check. Eligibility is based on the estate and your share, not your credit score, job, or income.
  • No monthly payments and no interest. The cost is agreed up front and does not grow while probate drags on.
  • No personal liability in a typical non-recourse advance. If the estate pays out less than expected, the funding company generally carries that risk. Learn more in do you have to pay back an inheritance advance.
  • Available before distribution. You do not need title in your name, and you generally do not need the executor's or other heirs' permission to assign part of your own share.

Quick rule of thumb: a bank lends against what you own now and asks you to repay no matter what. An inheritance advance is based on what you are expected to inherit, and the estate settles it.

Which option fits your situation?

  1. Strong credit, steady income, and no rush: a personal loan or HELOC on your own home may be cheaper, as long as you are comfortable with monthly payments and the risk to your home.
  2. Probate is almost done: waiting for distribution, then using the inherited property as collateral, may work.
  3. You need cash now, your credit is limited, or you do not want debt: an inheritance advance is often the better fit. See how it compares in inheritance advance vs. probate loan.

Inheritance loan FAQ

Can I get a bank loan against my inheritance?

Usually not while probate is open. Traditional banks generally lend based on your credit, income, and property you already own. An inheritance still in probate is not yours yet, so most banks will not accept it as collateral.

What bank options do heirs have during probate?

Heirs may qualify for regular credit on their own, such as an unsecured personal loan or a HELOC on a home they already own. These require credit approval and monthly payments regardless of how the estate turns out.

Can I borrow against an inherited house after probate?

Once the court orders distribution and the property is deeded into your name, you own it and can apply for a mortgage, refinance, or HELOC like any other owner, subject to normal lender approval.

Is an inheritance advance a loan?

No. An inheritance advance is an assignment of part of your expected share. There is no credit check, no monthly payment, and no interest, and with a non-recourse advance you generally do not owe the difference if the estate falls short.
