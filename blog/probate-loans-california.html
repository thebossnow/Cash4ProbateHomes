Probate Loans in California: How They Work and Who Qualifies

What lenders mean by a probate loan, who can actually get one, and the no-credit-check option most heirs end up using instead.

Published November 10, 2026

If you are waiting on an inheritance in California, you have probably seen ads for a probate loan. The phrase sounds simple, but it covers two very different products, and which one you are looking at decides who qualifies, what it costs, and who carries the risk. This guide walks through how probate loans work, who can get one, and what to ask before you sign anything.

What people mean by a "probate loan"

Online, "probate loan" is used for two separate things:

  1. A loan to the estate. The executor or administrator (the court calls this person the personal representative) borrows money on behalf of the estate, usually secured by real estate the estate owns. The estate is the borrower, and the loan is paid back from estate assets, often when a house sells.
  2. Funding for an individual heir. An heir gets cash based on their expected share of the estate. Some companies structure this as a true loan with interest. Many others, including Cash4ProbateHomes, structure it as an inheritance advance, which is not a loan at all.

Most people searching for "probate advance loans" or "probate loans near me" are heirs in the second group. They need money now, they are not in charge of the estate, and they cannot wait out a probate case that often runs 9 to 18 months or longer.

How a loan to the estate works

California Probate Code sections starting at 9800 allow a personal representative to borrow money and put a loan against estate property, generally with court approval. The executor files a petition explaining why the estate needs the money, such as paying off a maturing mortgage, covering property taxes, or making repairs before a sale. If the court agrees, the estate can take the loan.

A few things to know about this route:

  • Only the personal representative can do it. An heir who is not the executor cannot borrow on behalf of the estate.
  • The money serves the estate, not one heir. Estate loan funds go to estate expenses. They are not a way to put cash in a single beneficiary's pocket.
  • It is real debt. Interest accrues, the lender is secured by the property, and every heir's share shrinks by the cost of borrowing.
  • It takes court time. Petitions, notice, and hearings add steps before any money moves.

How heir loans and advances work

Funding for an individual heir is based on that heir's share of the estate. The company reviews the probate case, estimates what the heir is likely to receive, and offers cash now in exchange for being paid from that share when the estate distributes. California regulates these assignments of inheritance under Probate Code sections 11600 and following, and the court can review them.

The key difference is whether the product is a loan:

  • An heir loan is debt. Expect a credit check, interest that can grow the longer probate lasts, and personal responsibility to repay if the estate comes up short.
  • An inheritance advance is not debt. You assign part of your expected inheritance for a fixed amount agreed up front. There is no credit check, no monthly payment, and no interest. If the estate pays out less than expected, that risk generally sits with the funding company, not with you.

Read the label carefully: some companies market a product as a "probate loan" when it is actually an advance, and some call it an "advance" while charging fees that grow over time. Ask directly whether the cost is fixed, whether there is recourse to you personally, and whether there is a credit check.

Who qualifies for a probate loan in California

Qualification depends on which product you are applying for.

For a loan to the estate, the lender generally looks at:

  • Whether the borrower is the court-appointed personal representative
  • Whether the court has authorized the borrowing, where required
  • The value of the property securing the loan and how much is already owed on it
  • How and when the loan will be repaid, such as from a planned sale

For heir funding, the questions are usually about the estate and your share, not your personal finances:

  • You are a named beneficiary in the will or a legal heir under California law
  • There is an open probate case, or one is being opened, in a California county court
  • The estate has enough value left after debts, taxes, and expenses
  • Your expected share is large enough to support funding

With a true heir loan, a lender may also check your credit and income. With an inheritance advance, your credit score, job, and income do not decide eligibility, which is why advances work for heirs who would not qualify for traditional borrowing. Eligibility and amounts still vary case by case.

Probate loans near me: does location matter?

Probate is handled by the superior court in the county where the person lived, so the case number, county, and court filings are what a funding company reviews. You do not need a local office. Questions to ask before you sign

  1. Is this a loan or an assignment of my inheritance?
  2. Is the total cost a fixed amount, or does it grow if probate takes longer?
  3. Do I owe anything personally if the estate pays out less than expected?
  4. Are there any upfront, application, or hidden fees?
  5. Do you need the executor's or other heirs' permission?
  6. Will the agreement be filed with the probate court?

Probate loan FAQ

What is a probate loan?

The term covers two things: a loan the executor takes out on behalf of the estate, usually secured by estate property, and funding given to an individual heir against their expected share. Many heir products called probate loans are actually inheritance advances, which are not loans.

Can an heir get a probate loan if they are not the executor?

An heir cannot borrow on behalf of the estate, but an heir can usually get funding against their own share. With an inheritance advance, the executor's and other heirs' approval is generally not needed to assign part of your own share.

Do probate loans require a credit check?

True loans usually do. An inheritance advance does not, because eligibility is based on the estate and your share rather than your credit.

What happens if the estate is worth less than expected?

With a loan, you can still owe the balance. With a non-recourse inheritance advance, the funding company generally carries that risk, so you do not owe the difference.
